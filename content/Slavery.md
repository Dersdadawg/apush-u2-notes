==Racially-defined, lifelong, and hereditary chattel slavery of Africans became the dominant labor system of the plantation colonies over the course of the 17th century, replacing earlier reliance on English [[Indentured Servants]].==

## Why the Shift Happened
By the mid-1600s, improving economic conditions in England reduced the supply of willing servants, while [[Bacon's Rebellion]] (1676) showed planters the danger of a growing class of free, landless ex-servants. Enslaved Africans, by contrast, could be held for life with no promise of eventual freedom or land, and — unlike indentured servitude — their status could be passed to their children. Growing English access to the transatlantic slave trade, especially after the [[Royal African Company]]'s monopoly ended in 1698, made enslaved labor increasingly available.

## Legal Codification
Colonial assemblies wrote race-based, hereditary slavery into law across the late 1600s, borrowing heavily from the harsh [[Barbados Slave Code]]. Virginia's 1662 law that a child's status followed the mother's (rather than the father's, as under English common law) is a key example: it guaranteed that children born to enslaved women would themselves be enslaved for life.

## Regional Variation
- **Chesapeake** ([[Virginia]], [[Maryland]]): slavery grew gradually alongside [[Tobacco]] cultivation, becoming the majority labor system by the early-to-mid 1700s.
- **Lower South** ([[Carolinas]], [[Georgia]]): rice plantations adopted Barbadian-style slavery early and at large scale; enslaved people outnumbered white colonists in South Carolina by the early 18th century, and relative isolation let African traditions persist strongly (see [[Gullah]]).
- **Middle Colonies and New England**: slavery existed but on a smaller scale, more often in cities and skilled trades than on large plantations.

## The Middle Passage and Trade
Enslaved Africans reached the colonies via the [[Middle Passage]], the brutal Atlantic crossing that was one leg of the [[Triangular Trade]].

## Resistance
Enslaved people resisted through everyday acts, escape, and open revolt — most notably the [[New York City Slave Revolt 1712]] and the [[Stono River Revolt]] of 1739 in South Carolina — which led colonial legislatures to pass even harsher slave codes restricting enslaved people's movement, assembly, and access to literacy.
